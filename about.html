EMERGENCE

a digital ecosystem

Emergence is a zero-dependency artificial life simulation that runs entirely in your browser. Creatures with tiny neural networks are born into a world they know nothing about. They cannot see the code that governs them. They have no instructions, no objectives, no designer's intent. They have only a brain full of random noise, a body that burns energy, and the blind pressure of survival. Everything you see them do - the foraging, the hunting, the cooperation, the territorial clustering - is something they evolved on their own.

Nothing in this simulation is scripted. There is no pathfinding algorithm, no flocking rule, no decision tree. Each creature is a small neural network that takes in what it can sense and produces motor commands: which way to turn, how fast to move, whether to share energy, whether to seek a mate, how much chemical scent to leave behind. The creatures that find food survive. The creatures that find food and reproduce pass their brain weights to the next generation, slightly mutated. Over hundreds of generations, the population discovers strategies that no one programmed.

The Brain

Every creature carries a two-layer neural network. It has 32 inputs, an evolvable hidden layer (between 4 and 20 neurons, default 12), and 8 outputs. All activations use hyperbolic tangent. Four of the hidden neurons feed their activation back as input on the next tick - a simple form of recurrent memory that gives creatures the capacity to maintain internal state across time.

The brain processes once per simulation tick (60 times per second at 1x speed). Each tick, the creature perceives its surroundings, feeds the perception into its network, and acts on whatever the network outputs. There is no learning during a creature's lifetime. All adaptation happens across generations through mutation and selection.

The Genome

Beyond the brain, each creature carries a set of evolvable genes that determine its physical form and capabilities. These create genuine trade-offs: a large, fast creature with a big brain and wide sensory range is powerful but burns energy at a devastating rate. Evolution must balance investment across all of these axes.

  • hue Color lineage. Drifts slightly each generation. Determines species identity.
  • body size Physical scale (0.5x - 2.0x). Affects predation, energy cost, visual size.
  • speed Movement speed multiplier (0.5x - 2.0x). Faster = more ground covered, more energy burned.
  • brain size Number of hidden neurons (4-20). Bigger brains can represent more complex strategies but cost more to run.
  • sensory range How far the creature can detect food, other creatures, and obstacles (60-200 pixels). Wide vision is expensive.
  • diet Specialization between flora (near nutrient hotspots) and mineral (near rocks) food. A specialist harvests its preferred food efficiently but gets almost nothing from the other type.
  • pheromone rate Maximum chemical trail deposition rate (0.05-0.60). Loud trail-markers help kin navigate but burn energy and announce their presence to rivals. Silent creatures are cheap to run but leave no trace.

The World

The environment is not static. It creates varied selection pressures that reward different strategies in different places and times.

Nutrient hotspots are five invisible zones that slowly drift across the world. Flora food spawns in gaussian clusters around them. A creature that learns to follow food density is following these drifting sources, even though it cannot sense the hotspot directly - only the food it produces.

Rock formations are clusters of dark circles that block movement, pheromone diffusion, and food spawning. Mineral food spawns at their edges. Creatures that specialize in mineral diet evolve to navigate obstacle-dense regions.

Current zones are invisible drift forces that slowly push creatures in a direction. They move and rotate over time. Creatures have no direct sense of currents - they simply feel the push and must compensate through evolved motor control.

Day and night cycle over 60 seconds. Food spawns more frequently during the day. The visual atmosphere shifts from warm daylight to cooler night tones.

Seasons cycle over four day periods (about 4 minutes). Summer brings abundant food and stable hotspots. Winter brings scarcity and faster hotspot drift - food sources become unreliable. The trail canvas shifts from warm amber in summer to cool blue in winter.

Aging is invisible but relentless. After about 50 seconds of life, a creature's metabolism begins to increase. By 80 seconds, it has doubled. By 100 seconds, tripled. No creature lives forever. This forces generational turnover and accelerates evolution - creatures must reproduce before senescence, not just when convenient.

How Evolution Works Here

A creature reproduces when its energy exceeds a threshold. If its "mating" brain output is high and a willing mate is nearby with enough energy, sexual reproduction occurs: both parents' brain weights are mixed through uniform crossover (each connection randomly chosen from one parent or the other), genes are averaged, and the child is lightly mutated. If no mate is available, the creature reproduces asexually - cloning its brain with mutations.

Mutations are frequent and small. Each brain connection has a 12% chance of shifting by a small random amount per reproduction. Brain size itself can change by one neuron with 8% probability. Genes drift within their ranges. This is noisy, aggressive mutation - evolution needs to explore widely because the search space is enormous (a default-sized brain has over 450 connection weights, each a continuous value).

When the population drops below 21 creatures, a safety floor triggers: 85% of new creatures are mutated offspring of the best survivors, and 15% are fully random. They spawn near food sources. This prevents extinction spirals while preserving whatever evolutionary progress the population has made. The 15% random creatures provide genetic diversity - occasionally a random brain stumbles onto a strategy that the evolved population has not discovered.

The brain weights are initialized using Xavier/Glorot scaling, which keeps neurons in the linear regime of their activation function. This is critical: with 32 inputs and naive initialization, all neurons would saturate and mutations would have almost no effect on behavior. Xavier initialization creates genuine behavioral diversity in the initial population, giving natural selection something meaningful to work with from the first generation.

What Emerges

Within the first minute, creatures reliably evolve basic food-seeking: turning toward the nearest food and moving toward it. This is the first and easiest strategy to discover - the reward signal (eating = energy = survival = reproduction) is direct and immediate.

Over the next several minutes, more subtle behaviors appear. Creatures evolve dietary specialization - some lineages become flora specialists that forage near hotspots while others become mineral specialists that navigate the obstacle fields. The population splits into ecological niches with different spatial territories.

Cooperation is the most striking emergent behavior. The simulation includes a mutualism mechanic: creatures of the same species that are near each other and both have high "sharing" output receive a small energy bonus per tick. This is not altruism - both cooperators benefit directly. Over many generations, some lineages evolve to sustain high sharing output, forming visible clusters connected by teal cooperation lines. Other lineages evolve as solitary foragers with near-zero sharing, investing instead in speed, perception, or brain size. The simulation produces genuinely different strategies in different runs.

Predation creates drama. A creature that is 1.18x larger than a nearby creature can eat it, gaining energy. Nearby kin of the prey increase this threshold (safety in numbers), while nearby kin of the predator decrease it (pack hunting). This creates an arms race: prey species evolve to cluster for defense, predator species evolve to cluster for hunting power, and the balance between them shifts over evolutionary time.

Pheromone trails are functional. Benchmarking at long timescales confirmed that evolved brains develop significantly larger weights on pheromone inputs than would be expected by chance. Creatures use the chemical landscape for navigation - following kin trails toward productive areas, potentially avoiding foreign trails. The 8th brain output (pheromone deposition intensity) lets creatures choose when to mark and when to go silent, opening the door to strategic chemical communication.

Brain size evolves bidirectionally. Under conditions where cooperation is strong, brains often shrink - the cooperative foraging strategy is simple enough that large brains are metabolic waste. Under predation pressure, brains grow - detecting and avoiding threats requires more computation. This mirrors a real pattern in evolutionary biology: eusocial organisms often have simplified individual cognition.

The Eras

The simulation detects ecosystem-scale patterns and names them. The current era is displayed in the stats panel. These are not scripted events - they are emergent properties of the population's statistical state.

Cambrian Bloom
Five or more species coexist with viable populations
Dominion of [Species]
One species controls more than 55% of the population
Famine
Population is critically low
The Scholars
Average brain size has evolved above 13 neurons
Age of Giants
Average body size has grown beyond 1.65
The Swift
Average speed gene exceeds 1.4
Far Sight
Average sensory range exceeds 150

A typical run progresses from Cambrian Bloom (many species, none dominant) through competitive exclusion into Dominion (one species rises), then potentially into The Scholars or Age of Giants as evolution pushes traits upward. Famines occur during harsh winters or after population crashes. Each run tells a different story.

The Sound

A generative ambient drone plays continuously, built from oscillators and filtered noise using the Web Audio API. It responds to the ecosystem's state: the filter opens (brighter sound) when the population is healthy and diverse, the LFO quickens (subtle tension) when predation is high, and the resonance sharpens during famines. Individual events - births, feeding, deaths, predation - produce small sonic accents layered into the drone. At high simulation speeds, individual sounds are muted and only the atmospheric drone remains.

The most rewarding way to watch: run the simulation at 16x or 32x speed for a few minutes to let evolution advance past generation 50-100. Then slow to 1x, click a creature, and watch what it does. Watch how it moves relative to food, to other creatures, to obstacles. Compare it to a creature from a different species. The behaviors are subtle, but they are real - they are the product of thousands of generations of selection, encoded in connection weights that no human designed.

What This Is Not

This is not a game. There is no goal, no score, no win condition. It is closer to a digital aquarium or a generative art piece. The experience it offers is the same one you get from watching a nature documentary with the sound on: the slow recognition that the things moving on screen are not random, that there are patterns, that those patterns were not placed there by a designer but arose from the interaction of simple rules over time.

The moment this project chases is when a viewer looks at the screen and says: "Wait - did that creature just...?" That moment of recognition, where evolved behavior looks purposeful and intelligent, is what every feature in this simulation exists to serve.

Technical Notes

The entire simulation is a single HTML file loading 11 plain JavaScript files via script tags. No build tools, no bundler, no ES modules, no external dependencies. It runs from a file:// URL. Rendering uses Canvas 2D (no WebGL) to keep the code accessible. Audio uses the Web Audio API. The simulation maintains 60fps with 200+ creatures on modern hardware. All state lives in memory - save/load serializes to JSON. The source code is around 3,000 lines across 11 files.

Leave it running. Come back in ten minutes. Something will have changed.
